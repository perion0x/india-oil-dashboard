The case in four steps

  • India kept buying. Volume held through the Hormuz shock and, by September, was running slightly above the record year, because refiners swapped suppliers fast.
  • On paper, the slate flipped to Brent. The official Indian Crude Basket went from about a fifth Dated Brent to about three quarters in a few months.
  • Physically, it didn't. Most of the Brent-priced barrels were Russian Urals, a medium sour crude priced off Dated Brent. Product exports confirm it: jet exports fell and fuel oil exports rose, the opposite of what a lighter slate would do.
  • That creates new exposures. Indian refiners pay Brent-linked prices for Dubai-quality crude: a Brent–Dubai spread exposure, a bigger import bill, and a sourcing problem if Russian supply goes.

If the 65/35 basket were the real slate, refiners would make more jet and naphtha and less fuel oil. The export data shows the opposite. That is the evidence that the Brent share rose because Urals is priced off Brent, not because India bought lighter crude.

What if India stops buying Russian crude?

About — million b/d has to come from somewhere else. Which supplier fills it decides everything.

  • The Gulf replaces Russia (most likely). Saudi, Iraqi and UAE crude is close to Urals in quality, so refinery output barely changes. The basket's Brent share falls back toward its old level, and pricing lines up with quality again. More Asian buying of Gulf crude firms Dubai against Brent.
  • The Atlantic Basin replaces Russia. US, Brazilian, Guyanese and West African crude is lighter, so jet and petrol output rise and fuel oil and petcoke fall. This only works when Brent is cheap enough against Dubai for the cargoes to sail east.
  • Losing the discount costs India money. At a $10/bbl Urals discount, replacing Russian barrels costs about US$— billion a year.

Signal to watch: if the basket's Brent share and India's jet exports both rise, India is really switching to Atlantic crude. If the Brent share falls and jet stays flat, it's the Gulf.

Notes and sources

  • Tonnes are converted to barrels at 7.33 barrels per tonne; the true factor varies by grade. India's financial year runs April to March.
  • FY2026-27 data are provisional (PPAC file to September 2026). August–September use pro-rated customs data, which is why some product lines look alike across those months; earlier months have been revised as final data arrived. History comes from PPAC's FY2020-21 to FY2024-25 file and its separate FY2025-26 file.
  • The ATF export series excludes jet sold to foreign airlines in India. Reliance's export refinery dominates exports, and exports are what's left after domestic demand.
  • Supplier volumes are ship-tracking estimates (Kpler, via Business Standard and ThePrint). The September quality mix is an estimate from those shares and Platts grade data.
  • Refinery yields come from an indicative model (published assays from TotalEnergies, ExxonMobil and the Pearl Sweet prospectus; Nelson complexity by refinery). It runs 2–5 points high against PPAC refinery data. Urals and Arab Light have no free assay and use stand-ins.
  • Sources: PPAC (Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas) import/export, basket price, diesel and petrol FOB international prices, basket ratio, refining capacity, demand estimate and gas report; Mumbai Port Authority exchange rates; Platts Periodic Table of Oil (Sep 2026); Kpler; Platts for Urals differentials.
